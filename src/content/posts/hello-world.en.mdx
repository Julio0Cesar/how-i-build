## Why a post and not a case study

A case study is written after the fact. By then the alternatives have faded, and
what is left is the version that worked — which is the least interesting part.

A post is cheaper. It can be written while a decision is still uncomfortable,
before anyone knows whether it was right.

<Hypothesis>
Writing the reasoning down later would be roughly as accurate, because the
important parts are memorable.
</Hypothesis>

They are not. What survives a week is the conclusion; what disappears is the
option you rejected and the reason you rejected it. The conclusion alone reads
like it was obvious, and obvious conclusions teach nobody anything.

<Observation>
The notes worth keeping are the ones written before the outcome was known.
</Observation>

## What this section is for

That is the whole reason this section exists: some thoughts are not worth a case
study and are worth writing down.

## What a technical section looks like

The measure of the text is 68 characters, which is where reading is comfortable.
A block of commands or a table gets more room, because a chain of steps that
wraps is a chain you have to reassemble in your head:

<Experiment>
Remove the active toolchain and see which of the two tools complains first.
</Experiment>

```bash
$ which rustc
~/.cargo/bin/rustc
$ rustup toolchain uninstall stable
$ which rustc
~/.cargo/bin/rustc      # still there — it is a shim, not the compiler
```

| Piece | What it is | Where it lives |
|---|---|---|
| `rustup` | version manager | `~/.cargo/bin` |
| shim | a stand-in that forwards the call | `~/.cargo/bin` |
| `rustc` | the actual compiler | inside the toolchain |

And the chain the two of them actually form:

<Chain>
  <Step name="rustup">the version manager you install</Step>
  <Step name="shim">a stand-in on PATH that forwards the call</Step>
  <Step name="toolchain">the version the shim resolves to</Step>
  <Step name="rustc">the compiler that finally runs</Step>
</Chain>
